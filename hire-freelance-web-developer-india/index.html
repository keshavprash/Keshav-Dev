How to choose a freelance web developer in India

Most projects that go wrong were decided badly before any code was written — a vague brief, no way to verify the developer, and no agreement about who owns what. Here is how to get those three right, whoever you end up hiring.

About 9 minutes to read By Keshav Prashar, freelance React & MERN developer Last updated 30 August 2026

Freelancer, agency, or hire someone?

Decide this first, because it changes everything downstream.

Best whenThe trade-off
Freelancer One clear project, a defined budget, and you want to talk to the person writing the code One person means one calendar and one illness. Ask about availability honestly
Agency Multiple workstreams, design plus build, or you need contractual weight behind it Costs more, and the person you were sold by is rarely the person who builds it
In-house hire The software is your business and will change every week for years A salary, and a long hiring process, for work that may be a two-month project

For a single website or a first product version, a freelancer is usually the right shape. For something that has to be maintained by a team for five years, it usually is not.

Write the brief before you contact anyone

This is the highest-leverage half hour in the whole process. Without it, every quote you receive is priced against a different imagined project, and you cannot compare them.

Six lines is genuinely enough:

  1. What your business does, in one sentence.
  2. Who the site or app is for, and what you want them to do on it.
  3. The screens you think you need.
  4. Whether anyone logs in.
  5. Anything it must connect to — payments, WhatsApp, email, an existing system.
  6. Your rough budget and when you need it live.

Say your budget People hide it, worrying the number will simply be matched. The real cost of hiding it is three rounds of conversation before you find out nobody can build what you want for what you have. A stated budget gets you an honest yes, no, or "here is what fits" on day one.

How to check someone can actually build it

Anyone can claim a stack. These four checks are quick and hard to fake:

  • Read the source code. Ask for a public repository. You do not need to understand the code — look at whether commits are spread over time, whether files are organised, and whether the README explains what the project does. A portfolio with no readable code behind it is a set of screenshots.
  • Ask them to explain one decision. "Why did you use that database here?" A real builder answers with a trade-off. Someone who assembled a tutorial answers with an adjective.
  • Open their own site on your phone. On mobile data, not office wi-fi. Someone who cannot make their own site fast and usable on a phone will not make yours.
  • Watch the first reply. Did they answer your actual question, ask a sharper one back, or send a template? The first email predicts the project.

Questions worth asking before you commit

  • What exactly is included, and what is explicitly not?
  • Who owns the code, the domain and the hosting accounts when we are done?
  • How many rounds of changes are included, and what counts as a new request?
  • Who writes the content — and what happens to the timeline if I am late with it?
  • What happens if you are unavailable halfway through?
  • What does support look like after launch, and what does it cost?
  • Will I be able to make small changes myself?
  • How will I see progress before the end?

You are not testing their patience. You are finding out whether they have thought about the project as a working relationship or as a transaction.

Red flags

  • A quote with no questions asked. Nobody can price a project they have not understood. A fast number is a guess, and guesses get corrected later, at your expense.
  • Guaranteed Google rankings. Nobody controls Google's results. Anyone promising a position is either misinformed or counting on you not checking.
  • Everything is on their account. If the domain and hosting are in the developer's name, leaving means starting over. This is the most common way small businesses get trapped.
  • No source code, ever. "We host it for you" can be a fine service. "You cannot have the code" is a lock-in.
  • Full payment up front. An advance is normal and fair. The whole amount before anything exists is not.
  • Vague answers about maintenance. Every site needs changes. If nobody will say what that costs, assume it will be inconvenient.

How payment usually works

The common and reasonable structure in India for freelance project work is milestone based: an advance to start, one or more payments as agreed stages are delivered, and the balance on handover. Percentages vary — an advance somewhere between a quarter and a half is normal — but the principle does not: neither side should be carrying all the risk.

Get the scope, the milestones, the amounts and the deadline in writing before money moves. It does not need to be a formal legal contract for a small project, but it does need to be written down and agreed by both of you. Email counts.

What handover should include

Agree this at the start, not on the last day:

  • The complete source code, in a repository you can access.
  • Admin logins for the site and any services it uses.
  • Domain and hosting accounts in your name, with the passwords.
  • A short note on how to deploy a change and where things live.
  • A walkthrough — a call where you are shown how to use what you paid for.

What timelines are realistic

Be suspicious of anyone who gives you a delivery date before understanding the scope, in either direction — too fast is a warning, and so is a padded number that leaves room to disappear. A good developer will say "I will give you a timeline with the plan", then hold to the date they committed to in writing.

The single biggest cause of overrun on small projects is not development. It is content — text, photos and logos arriving weeks late. If you want it on time, have that ready before the build starts.
