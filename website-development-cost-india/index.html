How much does a website cost in India?

The honest answer is that it depends — but not on anything mysterious. It depends on a handful of decisions you can understand in ten minutes. Here is what actually drives the price, written by the developer who has to quote it.

About 9 minutes to read By Keshav Prashar, freelance React & MERN developer Last updated 30 August 2026

The short answer

Below are my own starting prices, and what sits inside each one. They are starting points for a small build — a real quote moves from here based on scope, which is what the rest of this page is about.

You will find cheaper in the Indian market, and you will find far more expensive. Both are usually explainable, and the last two sections of this page explain them.

What you are actually paying for

Almost nobody is billing you for pages. They are billing you for hours, and then presenting the total as a package. That distinction matters, because it tells you which questions change the price.

A five-page site where every page is the same layout with different text is one design and one build. A three-page site where each page is a different layout, with a booking form on one and a filterable listing on another, is three designs and three builds. The second is more expensive despite having fewer pages, and any developer who quotes it purely on page count is guessing.

The single most useful thing you can do Write down every screen your site needs and what a visitor does on each one. That document is what turns a vague range into a fixed number — from me or from anyone else you ask.

The eight things that move the price

In rough order of impact:

  1. Custom design versus a template. Adapting an existing theme is a fraction of the work of designing screens from nothing. It also constrains what the site can do, which is sometimes fine and sometimes the whole problem.
  2. Whether users log in. Accounts change everything. The moment you have users, you have registration, password reset, sessions, permissions and a place to store personal data safely. This is usually the single biggest jump.
  3. Whether you need to edit content yourself. Editable content means an admin area or a CMS behind the site. Real value, real cost.
  4. Number of genuinely different layouts, not number of pages.
  5. Integrations. Payments, WhatsApp, email, maps, a CRM, an existing system. Each one is somebody else's API to learn, handle failures from, and test.
  6. Who writes the content. If text and images arrive late or never, the project stalls and the cost of restarting it lands somewhere.
  7. How many rounds of changes are included, and what counts as a change rather than a new request.
  8. Timeline. Compressing a month of work into a week costs more, for the same reason it does in any trade.

Why a web application costs several times a website

People often use the two words interchangeably and are then surprised by the quote. The difference is not size, it is category.

Why two quotes for the same brief differ several times over

Usually one of four reasons, and none of them is anyone being dishonest:

  • Different reading of the same brief. A vague brief gets priced optimistically by one person and defensively by another. This is the most common cause by far, and it is fixable — by you, before you ask.
  • Template versus custom. A ₹5,000 quote and a ₹40,000 quote can both be fair if one is a theme with your logo dropped in and the other is a build.
  • Freelancer versus agency. An agency carries a designer, a project manager, an office and a sales cost. You may want exactly that. You are paying for it either way.
  • What is quietly excluded. Content, deployment, revisions, responsive testing and handover are the usual omissions. A cheap quote that excludes four of them is not cheap.

What a suspiciously cheap quote usually means

Sometimes it means someone is starting out and building a portfolio, which is a genuine and fair reason. Ask, and be told honestly, and you may get a good deal. Otherwise it usually means one of these:

  • A theme is being resold with the demo content replaced.
  • The scope is deliberately narrow, and everything you assumed was included becomes a paid change request later.
  • Hosting and the domain sit on the developer's account, so leaving means starting over.
  • The site is built once and abandoned — no handover, no source code, no support.

The defence is not paying more. It is asking three questions: what exactly is included, who owns the code and the accounts, and what happens after launch. There is more on this in how to choose a freelance web developer.

How to get a quote you can actually compare

Send every developer the same short brief. Six lines is enough:

  1. What your business does, in one sentence.
  2. Who the site is for, and what you want them to do on it.
  3. The screens you think you need.
  4. Whether anyone logs in.
  5. Anything it has to connect to — payments, WhatsApp, an existing system.
  6. Your rough budget and when you need it live.

Naming a budget is not weakness — it is how you find out fast whether someone can build what you want for what you have, instead of discovering it three conversations in. And if a developer cannot give you a fixed number after reading that, ask what is still unclear. The answer tells you a lot about how the project will run.
